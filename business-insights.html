What it really costs to not have a bookkeeper

Many business owners put off hiring a bookkeeper because it feels like one more bill. But doing the books yourself, or not doing them at all, has a cost too. It just doesn’t show up as a line on an invoice.

Your time has a price

Most owners who do their own books spend several hours a week on it, often at night. Say it’s five hours a week. That’s 260 hours a year. If your time is worth $75 an hour on a job or with a customer, that’s $19,500 a year spent on work someone else could do faster.

Missed receipts become missed deductions

Every business expense you can’t document is a deduction you may lose. If $5,000 in receipts never makes it into the books, you could pay roughly $1,000 to $1,800 more in tax, depending on your tax bracket and self-employment tax. Lost receipts add up quietly, one supply run at a time.

Pricing on a guess

Without job costing, many contractors price work on what feels right or what the competition charges. If your jobs are underpriced by just 5% and your business brings in $400,000 a year, that’s $20,000 left on the table. Clean books show you what each job really costs, so your next bid covers labor, materials, and overhead.

Late fees, penalties, and missed follow-ups

When nobody is watching the details, small things slip. A bill is paid late. A sales tax return is filed after the deadline. A customer invoice sits unpaid for 90 days because nobody followed up. Each one costs money, and some cost more the longer they sit.

The cleanup bill

Books that fall months behind don’t fix themselves. Catching up a year of records takes far more time, and money, than keeping them current each month. It also tends to happen at the worst time: right before taxes are due, or when a bank asks for financial statements.

What you get with a bookkeeper

  • Accounts reconciled every month, so your numbers match the bank
  • Clear reports that show profit, cash, and what’s owed
  • Job costs tracked so you can price with confidence
  • Organized records ready for your tax professional
  • Someone to ask when a number doesn’t look right

The honest question: Add up your hours, the receipts you can’t find, and the jobs you wonder about. For most owners, the cost of help is less than the cost of doing without it.

Your monthly bookkeeping review

Keeping your books current is more than entering transactions. A monthly review helps you spot missing details and resolve questions before they carry into the next month.

Start with complete records

Gather bank, credit card, and loan statements for the month. Include receipts, bills, and details for payments that need an explanation. If you use more than one payment system, check that the records are complete for each one.

Check balances and open items

  • Reconcile each bank and credit card account to its statement.
  • Review unpaid invoices and bills for old balances, duplicate entries, or payments applied to the wrong item.
  • Check loan balances and how payments were split between principal and interest.
  • Review uncategorized transactions and items waiting for an answer.

Read the reports together

Review your profit and loss report and balance sheet for the same period. Compare income and expenses with prior months. Ask about large changes or balances that don’t fit what happened in the business.

If you track projects, review the income and direct costs assigned to each job. A cost can be in the books but still be missing from the right project.

A useful monthly habit: Keep one list of open questions, who needs to answer them, and when you’ll follow up.

Profit and cash: why the numbers differ

A profitable month doesn’t always leave more money in the bank. Your profit and loss report shows income and expenses. Your bank balance shows the cash in that account at a point in time.

Timing can change the picture

On accrual reports, income can appear before the customer pays, and expenses can appear before you pay the bill. Check whether your reports use cash or accrual accounting before comparing them.

Some cash movements aren’t income or expenses

Loan proceeds add cash but aren’t sales. Paying loan principal reduces cash and the loan balance, rather than creating an expense. Transfers between bank accounts move money without changing profit. Owner contributions and distributions also affect cash without being ordinary sales or operating expenses.

Look beyond one number

Read your profit and loss report alongside the balance sheet. Review unpaid customer invoices, unpaid bills, loan activity, and owner transactions. Together, these explain more than the bank balance alone.

Ask a clear question: “What caused the change in cash this month?” Then trace the answer back to the records.
